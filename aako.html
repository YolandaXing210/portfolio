01 / Context

One project. Two distinct chapters.

The team began with a compelling idea: leave digital memories in the physical places where they happened.

We imagined AAKO as a location-based social platform. An “Echo” could hold a moment in space, allowing people to encounter memories through AR. The early business thinking explored how place, content, and social connection might reinforce one another.

In Chapter I, I developed the Echo 3D prototype as the product’s smallest spatial unit. Jinmao designed the mobile experience around that unit. After the first launch fell short of our expectations, Chapter II reframed the product and redesigned the mobile experience around the existing Echo.

Chapter I / Direction & spatial foundation

02 / Chapter I / Echo spatial prototype

Giving a memory a presence in space.

In Chapter I, I developed the Echo 3D prototype: the smallest spatial unit around which the mobile experience was designed.

The prototype explored a floating bubble containing visual media, with audio, text, and orbiting emoji reactions around it. The design question was how a memory could occupy a place while keeping its content and interactions understandable.

Echo was present from the beginning and remained the foundation in Chapter II. Jinmao’s original mobile design surrounded this unit; my later reframing redesigned those surrounding mobile pages. The prototype’s social reactions reflect the original direction, rather than evidence of successful public spatial interaction.

AR / Return to the place

The prototype explored an Echo anchored to the setting where a memory was created, with a reference image to support spatial alignment and positioning.

VR / Return from elsewhere

A remote viewing state explored placing the Echo within an environmental scene so a memory could be revisited without being physically at the location.

03 / Chapter II / Launch diagnosis

A compelling spatial idea did not become a reason to return.

After launch, usage was weaker than we expected. People were reluctant to engage, and many left during onboarding.

Our team was unusually comfortable sharing everyday life. We had carried that comfort into the product: if a memory mattered, perhaps people would want to place it in the world and let others respond. Real behavior challenged that assumption.

In a spatial product, a comment can carry an additional sense of presence. Discovering that someone has encountered a memory at a physical location may feel intrusive rather than welcoming. Trust could not be assumed just because the interaction was technically possible.

App analytics also helped us see where people abandoned the experience. The initial onboarding asked users to complete a strong, step-by-step AR introduction. Some devices were unsupported; for other users, the sequence asked for too much before they had experienced anything personally meaningful.

Evidence boundary: these are qualitative launch observations and the founder’s account of analytics. No specific conversion, retention, or abandonment rates are available here.

04 / Privacy & trust

The memories worth keeping were often the least “postable.”

A meaningful memory might be an awkward photo of a friend, an ordinary video, or a private joke. Its value comes from a relationship and a context, not from how well it performs in public.

Repeatedly asking “public or private?” turned saving a memory into an audience decision. The design problem extended beyond privacy controls: the product made people consider how they would be perceived before they could simply keep a moment.

I reframed the experience around the user and their closest friends. The goal was a clear, trusted context where sharing an imperfect moment would feel natural. That became a product principle; it is not a claim that privacy or trust had been fully validated.

What if saving a memory did not require performing for an audience?
05 / Research process

How does a moment come back?

I studied the interaction model itself: which cues help someone reconnect with a memory, and what does sound leave out?

This inquiry was distinct from Jinmao’s research into the early mobile app. I worked with participants’ own media to explore recollection, using an audio-first exercise and a related-image exercise. The purpose was to inform the spatial experience, rather than to measure a universal memory mechanism.

Study A / Audio + contextual prompts

Listen first. Add context gradually.

  1. Select a video at random from the participant’s own photo library.
  2. Play only its audio, withholding the video image.
  3. Ask what the participant remembers about the event.
  4. If recall is limited, introduce clues progressively: year, season, approximate time, and location.
  5. Observe which prompts help the person reconstruct the moment.

Study B / Related environment imagery

Show the setting, not the original video.

  1. Use photographs related to the same place or occasion.
  2. Show those images instead of the video itself.
  3. Ask whether the surrounding environment brings the event back.
  4. Consider what this suggests for an Echo that connects sound, imagery, and place.

A recalled example: a photograph of a beach helped bring back the experience of building a sandcastle with friends.

Each cue offers a different way back.

Audio

Sound can carry atmosphere without enough context.

Participants did not always reconstruct a moment from audio alone. That challenged our original idea of a purely audio-led AR experience.

Time / season

Time is remembered at different levels.

Season could offer a stronger association than an exact year in places with distinct seasons. Precise time mattered more in settings where it shaped the experience.

Place / environment

The setting can bring the event back.

Related environmental photos helped reconnect participants with an occasion, including the beach and sandcastle example. This informed a richer combination of imagery and place.

Recollection

Design for return, not a recall score.

Albums connect moments across people, place, and time. The design uses these observations to support revisiting; the study does not supply a quantified prediction of recall.

06 / Research findings

Context gives a memory somewhere to land.

The exploratory exercises suggested three useful directions. I treated them as design inputs, with room for further testing, rather than as statistically established findings.

01 / Visual & environmental

The surroundings can unlock the event.

Relevant photos and environmental cues helped reconnect a person to a moment. Location mattered as lived context—not simply as a coordinate on a map. Audio alone was not a sufficient anchor in the exercises.

02 / Temporal hierarchy

Season can be more meaningful than year.

Exact years were often fuzzy. In places with distinct seasons, spring, summer, autumn, or winter could produce a stronger response. The design should support how people remember time, not just how a database records it.

03 / Context-dependent precision

More precise does not always mean more useful.

Minute-level information added little in many situations. A time-specific setting, such as a bar, could make time of day more relevant. Context should determine which details deserve attention.

Study limits: participant count, prompt-by-prompt records, and formal scoring are not supplied. These observations do not establish cue effectiveness across populations. A subsequent study should vary cue order and compare recall quality without leading participants.

07 / Product reframe

From spatial social app to spatial memory system.

The Echo 3D prototype remained the smallest unit. The central question changed from “How do people share an Echo?” to “How do people keep, connect, and return to meaningful moments?”

The reframing focused on the mobile experience around Echo. A memory belongs to a trip, a season, a place, or a relationship. I introduced the album to connect existing Echo units and give the product a clearer reason to exist.

01

Intimacy before exposure.

Create a clear sharing context for the closest circle. Reduce the burden of deciding who should see each moment.

02

Connections before isolated posts.

Use albums to connect moments through place, time, people, and shared experience.

03

Personal meaning before instruction.

Let users begin with something they care about. Introduce spatial interaction after there is a memory worth interacting with.

04

Context before precision.

Combine visual and environmental cues with meaningful temporal information. Avoid making every detail equally prominent.

08 / Mobile redesign decisions

Make recollection the organizing idea.

The redesign reorganized the mobile experience around the existing Echo prototype. Three connected decisions carried the reframing into the phone interface: simplify visibility, make the album the organizing structure, and bring personal content into onboarding earlier.

Decision 01 / Audience

Four layers became one trusted context.

The original experience had four visibility layers. I reduced that structure to one layer, simplifying the home and navigation experience. The product direction emphasized the user and their closest friends, giving people a clearer expectation of who the experience was for.

Instead of treating every memory as an audience-management task, the redesign aimed to make saving feel less consequential.

Decision 02 / Information unit

From individual bubbles to an album of relationships.

An isolated bubble had to carry too much weight. An album allowed multiple moments to build meaning together, offering a sense of a journey rather than a collection of unrelated posts.

Place, season, shared people, and occasions became ways to connect memories. This moved the experience toward revisiting and accumulation: an ordinary moment could become meaningful as part of a larger history.

Decision 03 / First experience

Start with a memory, then invite interaction.

The initial onboarding demanded a guided AR sequence before the product had personal value. The redesign emphasized album creation and interaction with the user’s own content.

Users could put something meaningful into the system before exploring it. I also simplified Echo creation, bringing capture and contextual information into a shorter sequence. These changes addressed the observed burden; their effect on retention still needs measurement.

09 / Outcome & reflection

A clearer purpose. An impact still to validate.

What changed

The redesign gave AAKO a more specific product direction: intimate memory keeping and recollection. Albums connected moments; a simpler visibility structure reduced interface complexity; onboarding began with personally meaningful content.

The tangible outcome of Chapter II was a redefined product model and a redesigned mobile experience around the Echo unit developed in Chapter I. The interface explorations shown here document that shift.

What I can—and cannot—claim

I can explain the reasoning behind those decisions and show the design work. I cannot claim a measured increase in retention, trust, or recall from the evidence available in this case study.

The next validation should examine whether people complete their first album, understand its audience, feel comfortable keeping imperfect moments, and return voluntarily to revisit them. Recall studies should compare cue combinations and cue order.

My biggest learning: a novel interaction is only valuable when it supports a human reason to use it.

Building Echo helped me explore what spatial media could do. Revisiting AAKO’s positioning helped me ask what it should do. The stronger direction came from reconsidering the social assumption and designing around how people protect, connect, and return to their memories.
