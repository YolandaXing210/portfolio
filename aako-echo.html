01 / Context

Leave a memory in the place where it happened.

AAKO started from a road-trip habit. The founder and I have been friends since middle school and have traveled across many countries together. A trip pulls you out of ordinary life, and that displacement makes places stick: landscapes and moments become personal and memorable.

Modern tools make it easy to capture everything, but they strip memories of context. Flattened into chronological feeds, moments lose where they happened and how they felt; locations become metadata. The team imagined AAKO as a way to leave digital memories in the physical places where they happened, and to encounter them again through AR.

Within that, I developed Echo: the 3D form a memory takes in space.

02 / Concept

No competitor had an actual entity of what a post is.

I looked at apps that touch on place, memory, and sharing—Google Maps, Instagram, Yik Yak, Yelp, and others. Most popular social platforms were released in the early 2000s, and they all rely on linear thread models grouped by topic or user. A post is a row in a feed, not a thing.

The design goal was to create a 3D post that combines images, sound, and text with a physical form, so expression can move beyond the screen. By occupying space and interacting with the body, it should extend digital communication into the physical world.

Visual inspiration

Mystery solving and conquering · a sense of achievement · reflecting the object and its surroundings · dreamy, childhood, nostalgia

03 / Research process

How does a moment come back?

I studied the interaction model itself: which cues help someone reconnect with a memory, and what does sound leave out?

This inquiry was distinct from Jinmao’s research into the early mobile app. I worked with participants’ own media to explore recollection, using an audio-first exercise and a related-image exercise. The purpose was to inform the spatial experience, rather than to measure a universal memory mechanism.

Study A / Audio + contextual prompts

Listen first. Add context gradually.

  1. Select a video at random from the participant’s own photo library.
  2. Play only its audio, withholding the video image.
  3. Ask what the participant remembers about the event.
  4. If recall is limited, introduce clues progressively: year, season, approximate time, and location.
  5. Observe which prompts help the person reconstruct the moment.

Study B / Related environment imagery

Show the setting, not the original video.

  1. Use photographs related to the same place or occasion.
  2. Show those images instead of the video itself.
  3. Ask whether the surrounding environment brings the event back.
  4. Consider what this suggests for an Echo that connects sound, imagery, and place.

A recalled example: a photograph of a beach helped bring back the experience of building a sandcastle with friends.

Each cue offers a different way back.

Audio

Sound can carry atmosphere without enough context.

Participants did not always reconstruct a moment from audio alone. That challenged our original idea of a purely audio-led AR experience.

Time / season

Time is remembered at different levels.

Season could offer a stronger association than an exact year in places with distinct seasons. Precise time mattered more in settings where it shaped the experience.

Place / environment

The setting can bring the event back.

Related environmental photos helped reconnect participants with an occasion, including the beach and sandcastle example. This informed a richer combination of imagery and place.

Recollection

Design for return, not a recall score.

The design uses these observations to support revisiting; the study does not supply a quantified prediction of recall.

04 / Research findings

Context gives a memory somewhere to land.

The exploratory exercises suggested three useful directions. I treated them as design inputs, with room for further testing, rather than as statistically established findings.

01 / Visual & environmental

The surroundings can unlock the event.

Relevant photos and environmental cues helped reconnect a person to a moment. Location mattered as lived context—not simply as a coordinate on a map. Audio alone was not a sufficient anchor in the exercises.

02 / Temporal hierarchy

Season can be more meaningful than year.

Exact years were often fuzzy. In places with distinct seasons, spring, summer, autumn, or winter could produce a stronger response. The design should support how people remember time, not just how a database records it.

03 / Context-dependent precision

More precise does not always mean more useful.

Minute-level information added little in many situations. A time-specific setting, such as a bar, could make time of day more relevant. Context should determine which details deserve attention.

05 / Anatomy of an Echo

A floating bubble that makes a memory alive.

The design question was how a memory could occupy a place while keeping its content and interactions understandable. Each part of the Echo carries one kind of content.

Bubble shape

A floating bubble that makes the memory feel alive.

Visual content

Images and video inside the bubble, viewable from different angles.

Textual content

User info and caption above, with activity emoji to enhance search.

Audio content

Sound from a recording, the video, or music.

Emoji orbit

Other people’s reactions orbiting the memory.

The purple bubble is the default. With the same structure, the team considered many more bubble skins with different textures and features that users could purchase.

06 / Rendering

Intimate rather than schematic.

I tested multiple engines and pipelines, including Niantic Lightship, Unity, and Apple ARKit. Lightship performs well for large-scale outdoor tracking, but its visual output felt too utilitarian for AAKO, with limited control over material softness and lighting nuance. RealityKit offered better visual fidelity and tighter integration with iOS hardware, letting memories feel intimate rather than schematic.

From prototype to product

Landing the bubble with engineering

Designing the bubble was half the work; the other half was getting it built. Our engineering team wrote strong front-end and back-end code, but 3D was unfamiliar territory: how things actually work inside a 3D scene wasn’t obvious from a 2D app background.

I used my 3D modeling knowledge to bridge that gap, explaining the bubble in terms of the parts it’s made of:

Mesh

The geometry of the bubble: the surface its content is mapped onto.

Normals

Which way each part of that surface faces, which decides how light and reflections fall across it.

Shaders

How the surface is drawn—its softness, transparency, and sheen—and why a node shader lets one bubble take many skins.

Direction

Orientation in space: which way the Echo, its caption, and the light are facing as the viewer moves around it.

Masking

The masks that fit an image or video inside the bubble and soften its edges into the surroundings.

With that shared vocabulary, the engineers could reason about the bubble themselves instead of treating it as a black box, and the soft, intimate Echo from the prototype made it into the app.

08 / What came next

The form held. The social premise didn’t.

The prototype’s social reactions reflect the original direction—a spatial social app—rather than evidence of successful public spatial interaction. After launch, usage was weaker than expected, and that premise was the part that needed rethinking.

Echo itself stayed. It remained the smallest spatial unit when I reframed AAKO from a spatial social app into a spatial memory system and redesigned the mobile experience around it.
